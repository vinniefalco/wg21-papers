## 1. Starting points

### Axioms

1. **Axiom.** Attending a WG21 meeting in person costs money and time.
2. **Axiom.** A sponsor can pay a participant's travel and salary, but cannot attend, speak, or vote in the participant's place.
3. **Axiom.** A participant who attends most meetings expects to meet many of the same colleagues at future meetings. A chair expects fewer problems from advancing a paper whose author has a record of following the procedures and responding to review.
4. **Axiom.** Given a choice, and other things equal, each participant, sponsor, national body, and user prefers the option that brings it more of what it values, sooner, at less cost to it, and weighs a gain in one value against a loss in another on one scale of its own. For this proof, a participant values adoption of the proposals they support, the interests of their sponsor if they have one, their standing, keeping any chair or convenor post they hold, their time and money, and a good result for users; a sponsor values its interests, which can depend on particular features of a design; a national body values the outcomes its national position supports and, where it has none yet, the interests concerned at its national level; and a user values the features they use or would use, how well those features work, and their own time and money.
5. **Axiom.** A participant who votes Against a colleague's proposal cannot rule out that the colleague will support the participant's own proposals less readily. A Neutral vote, or not voting, gives the colleague no more reason for this than an Against vote does.
6. **Axiom.** No participant can study every paper closely, so each chooses which papers to study, and what a participant learns from a paper or its discussion can change their vote.
7. **Axiom.** Writing or reviewing a paper takes time, and, other things equal, a longer or more complex paper takes more; preparing to give reasons for a vote also takes time.
8. **Axiom.** Until a design is implemented and used, nobody can be sure how well it works, and even then those voting on it cannot fully judge how well it serves users. A design with more parts and more interactions with existing wording has more places where a defect can go unnoticed.
9. **Axiom.** When a proposal is first presented, it has received less committee review than at any later meeting. When a design is presented in parts, reviewers of an early part cannot study in detail what the later parts will require of it until those parts are presented.
10. **Axiom.** If a decision is reversed, the work built on it must be redone or discarded. After a favorable poll, authors and others build more on the design, such as wording, implementations, and proposals that depend on it.
11. **Axiom.** Revising a design throughout costs its authors more work than a change confined to one part of it.
12. **Axiom.** A participant learns how the committee works partly by attending, some of it only that way, and this knowledge differs from skill at design. Participants can use only procedural options they know, and, other things equal, those who know more spend less effort getting a paper considered and have a better chance of adoption.
13. **Axiom.** Over the years, some participants stop attending and others join, while a published paper stays available to later readers for decades. Someone absent from a meeting learns what was said there only from writing or from those present who remember it, and a clear written objection is easier to raise again.
14. **Axiom.** A meeting has a fixed number of hours, so time given to one paper is not available to another.
15. **Axiom.** WG21 plans a new edition of the C++ standard every three years, and ISO publishes each edition once national bodies approve it.
16. **Axiom.** When several subgroups meet at the same time, a participant can attend only one of them.
17. **Axiom.** A design in the standard applies to every user of its feature, so a better design's gain is spread across them all, while participating costs the participant or their sponsor. Users differ from each other and from participants in the features they care about and what they can spend taking part, and one user's effort seldom changes a committee outcome.

### Definitions

18. **Definition.** A participant is a registered expert attending a WG21 meeting. Guests a convenor invites are not participants.
19. **Definition.** A regular is a participant who expects to attend future meetings with many of the same colleagues.
20. **Definition.** A newcomer is a participant attending for the first time; a newcomer who expects to attend future meetings with many of the same colleagues is also a regular.
21. **Definition.** An author is a participant who presents a proposal paper as one of its authors.
22. **Definition.** A sponsor is an organization that pays some or all of a participant's costs of taking part.
23. **Definition.** A user is a person who writes C++ and does not participate.
24. **Definition.** A design room is EWG or LEWG, a study group is any WG21 study group, a wording room is CWG or LWG, and a subgroup is any of these. Plenary is a WG21 meeting's closing plenary session, a chair chairs a subgroup, the convenor convenes WG21, and a consensus call is a chair's or the convenor's determination of consensus.
25. **Definition.** A national body is an ISO member body that is a P-member of SC22, the committee WG21 belongs to. In quotations from the Directives, "the committee" is SC22, "the Chair" its chair, and "National Body" any ISO member body, which on enquiry and final ballots includes non-P-members of SC22.
26. **Definition.** Favor, Neutral, and Against are the poll positions as SD-4 capitalizes them, with Strongly Favor and Weakly Favor counting as Favor and likewise for Against. The 2:1 norm is SD-4's statement on subgroup polls that a proposal normally advances when, after discussion of the concerns of those voting against, more than twice as many are in favor as against, even if many vote Neutral, though a Neutral majority of those voting can be concerning.
27. **Definition.** A participant's standing is the readiness of colleagues to support the participant's future proposals.
28. **Definition.** A player's stake in a poll is the difference the poll's outcome makes to what the player values.
29. **Definition.** A statement that a player does better with one option than another means the option is worth more to the player, by their weighing of the values the statement names. Every value the statement does not name is held equal.
30. **Definition.** A direction poll is a poll on whether to continue work on a design, and a forwarding poll is a poll to send a proposal to the next room.
31. **Definition.** An incumbent on a topic is a design that has passed a direction poll in a design room and has not failed a later direction poll there.
32. **Definition.** An edition is one published version of the C++ standard, and its feature cutoff is the last meeting at which a new feature can be approved for it.
33. **Definition.** A reversal is the removal or replacement of a design or decision that WG21 or one of its subgroups has approved.
34. **Definition.** A player is a person or body whose choices the proof considers. A settled pattern, which game theory calls a Nash equilibrium, is a set of choices, one per player, in which no player gains by changing only their own choice.
35. **Definition.** A small proposal is a proposal by one author who pays their own costs and expects its design to be reviewed within one meeting.
36. **Definition.** A large proposal is a proposal whose two or more authors have their costs paid by one or more sponsors and expect its review to span more than one edition. Its paper is longer and more complex than the paper of any small proposal.
37. **Definition.** A result-seeking author is an author whose gain from a proposal depends on how well the adopted design serves its users, not on whether it keeps any particular feature.
38. **Definition.** A design-bound author is one whose gain from a proposal depends on the adopted design keeping particular features fixed before review, for example because a sponsor's interests depend on them.

## 5. Momentum: incumbents and the cost of reversal

81. **Induction.** Each decision built on a design adds work that reversing the design would redo or discard, and more is built after each favorable poll. So the cost of reversing a design grows as it advances, whether or not the design is wrong. *[from 10, 4, 33, 43r, 43u, 44d]*
82. **Proposition.** A design passing a direction poll becomes the incumbent, and a rival without a paper cannot block it, though the chair may delay it one meeting for one to arrive. A rival with a paper can be polled alongside it, the room can direct changes, and by default a TS merge proceeds with it unless an alternative is actively progressing. *[from 42a, 42k, 42l, 43q, 43d, 42n, 31, 30]*
83. **Proposition.** A design can become the incumbent at its first direction poll, when it has had less review than at any later meeting. From then on, every rival and every later change to it bears a reversal cost that grows as more is built on it. *[from 9, 8, 31, 30, 82, 81, 33]*
84. **Proposition.** A rival wins a voter from an incumbent only with an advantage worth more to that voter than their share of the reversal's cost. That hurdle grows with each decision built on the incumbent, which faces no such hurdle itself. *[from 4, 81, 82, 31, 33, 29]*
85. **Lemma.** A direction poll asks voters only to agree that work should continue, less than a forwarding poll asks, so it can pass where a forwarding poll would fail. Yet passing it makes the design the incumbent. *[from 30, 31, 43r, 4, 83]*
86. **Proposition.** If a poll places a proposal among the work the committee aims to include in the next IS, the proposal joins a category SD-4 has chairs take up first. A meeting's hours are fixed, so the time it then gets comes out of the time left for papers outside those categories. *[from 43v, 14]*
87. **Proposition.** A longer, more complex design costs more to study and has more places where a defect can go unnoticed. Yet a reviewer whose stake does not grow with its size does better studying it less closely than a smaller design with the same stake. *[from 7, 6, 8, 4, 28, 29]*
88. **Proposition.** Participants who do not study a proposal typically abstain, so one costing more to study than most stakes in it is voted on mainly by those with large stakes. Unless those of them who object cast at least a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes, it can advance with no change at all. *[from 43g, 87, 7, 4, 63, 73]*
89. **Proposition.** A design normally needs the 2:1 norm to advance, and once adopted, a paper or ballot comment to remove it normally needs the same, switching the default to keeping it. After a successful final ballot, design changes are expected to have near-unanimous consent, ISO bars technical changes at approval, and SD-4 calls published features much harder to update incompatibly. *[from 43f, 42a, 47e, 47j, 54c, 45g, 33]*
90. **Proposition.** An objector who arrives after a design has been adopted faces three obstacles at once. Removal needs a paper meeting the 2:1 norm, reversal costs more for every decision built on the design, and the record keeps its case but unwritten objections only in memory. *[from 89, 81, 84, 76, 78]*

## 11. The settled pattern

125. **Lemma.** Whatever the others choose, a regular does no better than to vote Favor on colleagues' proposals they support and, on the rest, to vote Neutral or not at all unless a doubt is worth more than an Against vote's extra cost. That cost is the risk to colleagues' support and the time for reasons, and the vote changes the result only when the count sits one or two votes above the line. *[from 64, 65, 63, 66, 67, 68, 77, 119, 62, 34]*
126. **Lemma.** Whatever the others choose, a design-bound author does no better than to seek direction polls as early as they can pass, propose first the smallest part that fixes the features its gain depends on, and seek the IS over a TS unless the room requires one. It does no better than to leave out trade-offs no reviewer would raise, ask for no poll against a rival it expects to lose, and answer objections with the cheapest change that keeps the 2:1 norm met, leaving those features alone unless the room directs otherwise. *[from 83, 81, 84, 85, 95, 94, 120, 116, 73, 69, 74, 92, 43q, 31, 38, 62a, 34]*

138. **Theorem.** Under SD-4 and the quoted clauses of ISO/IEC Directives Part 1, in the edition replacing the JTC 1 Supplement, and given the axioms, these choices form a settled pattern. In it, no player improves their position by changing only their own choice. A design-bound author seeks direction polls as early as they can pass, proposes first the smallest part fixing the features it needs, and seeks the IS over a TS. It leaves out trade-offs no reviewer would raise and answers objections with the cheapest change that keeps the 2:1 norm met. A result-seeking author states trade-offs and makes changes whenever the better design they bring is worth their cost to it. It backs a rival that serves users better by more than its share of the reversal's cost. An author who values both chooses, at each two-way choice, as the type whose value differs more. A small proposal's author keeps it presented while it is worth costs borne alone. A regular votes Favor on colleagues' proposals they support and otherwise Neutral or not at all, voting Against only for a doubt worth more than that vote's extra cost. A sponsor pays for participants where the decisions it values are made, study groups included. Chairs and the convenor follow SD-4's procedure unless their stake outweighs what departing can cost them. A national body votes Yes with comments unless blocking one feature is worth as much to it as the rest of the document. A user takes part only when their own stake outweighs a cost borne alone, and otherwise uses a cheaper move or none.

     In this pattern a design's content can be fixed when it is least reviewed. Its first favorable poll can make it the incumbent, and every later change or rival then pays a reversal cost that grows whether or not the design is wrong. Objections count only in the forms and times that attending teaches. In a long deadlock, the group whose sponsors pay its costs can hold longest, whichever design is better. A proposal that costs more to study than most participants' stakes in it can advance with no change at all. An objector who arrives after adoption meets these obstacles together. Other things equal, a proposal advances more readily when its authors keep presenting it or know the procedures, or when a sponsor pays for participants who hold its position. The same holds when the chair expects fewer problems from it, a poll has placed it among the work aimed at the next IS, or it is already the incumbent. No user votes, and the needs that reach a vote are those participants and their sponsors know, which need not match those of users at large. The participants present decide content, and a sponsor can add voters without limit. A change to a design costs the sponsors who have built on it, while its gain is spread across all users. An author gains at adoption, while a hidden flaw costs users later. Technical merit enters through what participants value, including a good result for users, and through the discussion, papers, and implementation experience that inform their votes. SD-4 assigns no one to rule on it, and the consensus test never asks whether an objection is right. No step checks whether separately adopted designs work together, review whose gain is spread across all users goes undone, and the record need not show whether objections were answered. The rules ask no author to state a trade-off. Leaving one out costs a design-bound author nothing the rules impose, while stating one can cost a result-seeking author votes. A first part that fixes a structure is reviewed before anyone can study what the later parts need of it, and every rival must then replace it. The rules also contain checks on each of these effects, and each result about large or small proposals holds for any proposal with the attributes it rests on. *[from 125, 126, 127, 128, 129, 130, 131, 132, 133, 134, 135, 136, 137, 59, 82, 83, 81, 84, 79, 88, 90, 86, 105, 102, 108, 109, 91, 92, 72, 110, 112, 111, 114, 113, 70, 116, 95, 98, 61, 51a, 58f, 42h, 12, 4, 34]*
