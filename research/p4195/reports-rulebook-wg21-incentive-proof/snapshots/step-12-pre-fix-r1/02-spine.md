## 3. What a vote costs

1. **Lemma.** Under the 2:1 norm each Against vote raises by two the Favor votes a proposal needs, while Neutral votes and abstentions leave the count unchanged. One Against vote changes the result only at one or two votes above the line, and at least a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes cast Against keeps the norm unmet. *[proved by l:against-double, l:neutral-outside, l:room-size, l:one-third-against]* {#l:vote-count}
   1. **Lemma.** Under the 2:1 norm, each Against vote raises by two the number of Favor votes a proposal needs. *[from f:two-to-one, d:positions]* {#l:against-double}
   1. **Lemma.** The 2:1 comparison counts only Favor and Against votes, though SD-4 says a Neutral majority of those voting can be concerning. *[from f:two-to-one, f:not-vote, d:positions]* {#l:neutral-outside}
   1. **Lemma.** One more Against vote changes whether the 2:1 norm is met only when the Favor votes exceed twice the other Against votes by one or two. *[from f:two-to-one, d:positions]* {#l:room-size}
   1. **Lemma.** Any group casting at least one third of the Favor-plus-Against votes as Against prevents the 2:1 norm from being met. *[from f:two-to-one, d:positions]* {#l:one-third-against}
1. **Proposition.** For a regular, an Against vote on a colleague's proposal costs more than a Neutral vote or none. It risks the colleague's later support no less, and SD-4 tells only Against voters to be ready to give reasons, which take time to prepare. *[from ax:support, ax:repeat, ax:prefer, ax:review-cost, f:against-reasons, f:no-reasons-other, d:regular, d:standing]* {#p:against-costs-more}
1. **Proposition.** On a colleague's proposal they doubt, a regular does better voting Against only when the change that vote can make outweighs the standing and time it costs. One Against vote changes the result only when the count sits one or two votes above the line, though it can also win changes to the design. *[from l:vote-count, p:against-costs-more, ax:prefer, f:against-reasons, f:direction-implement, d:stake, d:does-better]* {#p:doubt-outside-count}
1. **Proposition.** With many Favor votes cast, one objector's Against vote changes the result only if enough others also vote Against, so its worth to each objector depends on the others' votes. The same objectors can then form either of two settled patterns: just enough of them vote Against to block, or none does. *[from l:vote-count, p:doubt-outside-count, p:against-costs-more, d:stake, d:settled-pattern]* {#p:objector-coordination}
1. **Proposition.** A participant can argue for another design and still vote Neutral, which leaves the count unchanged and, unlike Against, is a vote SD-4 asks no one to be ready to explain. *[from f:negative-welcome, l:vote-count, f:against-reasons, f:no-reasons-other]* {#p:prefer-vs-stop}
1. **Proposition.** Two regulars with pending proposals each risk the other's support for their own by opposing the other's proposal. When each one's doubt is worth less than that risk and the time for reasons, each does better refraining whatever the other does, and both do better than if both opposed. *[proved by l:mutual-refrain, l:mutual-own-vs-other]* *[from ax:support, ax:prefer, p:against-costs-more, p:doubt-outside-count, l:vote-count, d:does-better]* {#p:mutual-support}

## 4. What the record keeps and how objections count

1. **Proposition.** A proposal can advance through WG21 with objections unresolved, since it needs consensus calls, under the 2:1 norm in the rooms and by the convenor at plenary, not every objector's persuasion. *[proved by l:address-not-resolve, p:advance-unanswered]* {#p:persuasion-not-required}
   1. **Lemma.** Under ISO, the obligation to address a sustained opposition is no obligation to resolve it, and a sustained opposition is not a veto. Still, ISO's general principles say consensus requires the resolution of substantial objections. *[from f:not-veto, f:resolve-substantial]* {#l:address-not-resolve}
   1. **Proposition.** A proposal can advance while discussed objections to it remain unresolved, because it advances on consensus calls, under the 2:1 norm in the rooms and by the convenor at plenary, and consensus need not imply unanimity. *[from l:address-not-resolve, f:design-forward, f:wording-forward, f:plenary-owns, f:two-to-one, f:consensus-def]* {#p:advance-unanswered}
1. **Proposition.** The record SD-4 and the searched ISO clauses require of a WG21 poll need not show whether an objection was answered or outvoted, or why consensus was called over it. *[proved by l:record-content, l:record-checks, l:iso-register-level]* *[from f:iso-no-reason-record]* {#p:record-indistinct}
   1. **Lemma.** SD-4 requires minutes and lets poll questions and numeric results be quoted publicly, but requires no record of how an objection was answered or why a consensus call was made. Plenary may ask a subgroup chair to summarize the reason for a decision. *[from f:minutes, f:protected, f:no-reason-record, f:plenary-reason]* {#l:record-content}
   1. **Lemma.** ISO lets participants comment on or object to minutes before they are confirmed, and has the chair of SC22 see that points of view at SC22's meetings are summarized. It bars participants from commenting publicly on others' contributions. *[from f:minutes-objections, f:views-summarized, f:no-public-comment]* {#l:record-checks}
   1. **Lemma.** The chair of SC22 judges consensus at SC22's committee stage, and ISO 2.5.7 recommends steps for a sustained opposition, including registering it, to the leadership. It does not say whether a convenor applies them inside a working group, and under either reading SD-4 adds no step for assessing or registering an opposition in subgroup polls. *[from f:chair-judges, f:pmember-consensus, f:committee-stage, f:iso-register, f:leadership-judges, f:sustained-def, f:no-sustained-procedure, d:national-body]* {#l:iso-register-level}
1. **Proposition.** SD-4 adopts ISO's definition of consensus, marked by the absence of sustained opposition, but names no step for judging or registering one, nor criteria for an important part of the concerned interests. Whoever ISO's leadership is for a working group, SD-4's own steps for an objection include the 2:1 norm, Against voters' reasons on request, escalation, and plenary's 3-way poll and national-position question. *[from f:wg-consensus, f:consensus-def, f:sustained-def, f:two-to-one, f:against-reasons, f:escalate-deadline, f:plenary-consent, f:plenary-nb, f:iso-register, f:no-sustained-procedure]* {#p:definition-without-procedure}
1. **Lemma.** Under SD-4's definition, an objection prevents consensus as a sustained opposition only if it concerns a substantial issue, is maintained, and comes from an important part of the concerned interests. The leadership judges that part case by case, none of the three conditions asks whether the objection is right, and the process must seek to reconcile conflicting arguments. *[from f:consensus-def, f:sustained-def, f:concerned-interests, f:majority-listen, f:views-heard]* {#l:consensus-test}
1. **Proposition.** When an objection's Against votes are too few to stop the 2:1 norm, the cheapest response keeping it met at that poll is no change or a partial change. The count measures votes, not whether the objectors are right, and SD-4 expects any change the room directs to be made or defended with new information. *[from ax:prefer, ax:redesign-cost, ax:review-cost, l:vote-count, f:direction-implement]* {#p:least-cost-response}
1. **Proposition.** SD-4 asks an Against voter to be ready to name the changes that would win them over, or to say that none would. Concerns stated as changes give authors items to grant one at a time, while opposition in any form offers none and counts in the poll only as a vote. *[from f:against-reasons, m:moves-author]* {#p:change-list}
1. **Proposition.** Of minutes, non-public email lists, records of subgroup discussion, ballot comments, and papers, only a paper puts an objection's argument outside the password-protected materials without anyone else's help. Writing one takes time. *[from p:record-indistinct, f:protected, f:minutes-objections, f:comments-national, f:late-info, f:escalate-paper, m:moves-regular, ax:review-cost]* {#p:dissent-needs-paper}
1. **Proposition.** A proposal's case lasts for decades in its required paper, while an unwritten reason survives only in the memory of those who heard it. Outside the protected records, an objection's argument outlasts its hearers only in writing, such as the objector's paper or an author's paper showing the alternative or defending a declined change. *[from f:no-paper, ax:turnover, p:dissent-needs-paper, p:record-indistinct, f:show-alternatives, f:direction-implement]* {#p:reasons-unrecorded}
1. **Proposition.** SD-4 describes an objector who presses on after a consensus call, without new information or allowed escalation, as not accepting a group decision, whether or not anyone answered it. It says the same of a ballot comment repeating an objection decided where its national body was present and heard, unless the comment brings new information or the body warned all along that it would vote No. *[from f:minority-accept, f:accept-decisions, f:escalation-allowed, f:timely, f:ballot-decided, f:ballot-new-info, f:no-surprise]* {#p:repeat-objection}
1. **Proposition.** SD-4 gives an objection weight at plenary only if its holder escalated it by 5pm the evening before, on the email lists and within their national body. ## 5. Incumbents and the cost of reversal

1. **Induction.** Each decision built on a design adds work that reversing the design would redo or discard, and more is built after each favorable poll. So the cost of reversing a design grows as it advances, whether or not the design is wrong. *[from ax:rework, ax:prefer, d:reversal, f:design-forward, f:wording-forward, f:plenary-owns]* {#ind:reversal-grows}
1. **Proposition.** A design passing a design room's direction poll becomes the incumbent, a paperless rival cannot block it, and the chair may delay it one meeting for a paper to arrive. A rival with a paper can be polled alongside it, the room can direct changes, and by default a TS merge proceeds with it unless an alternative is actively progressing. *[from f:no-paper, f:no-wait-alternatives, f:explore-alternatives, f:direction-implement, f:multiple-polls, f:ts-default, d:incumbent, d:polls]* {#p:incumbent-start}
1. **Proposition.** A design can become the incumbent at its first direction poll, when it has had less review than at any later meeting. From then on, a rival or a later change must redo or discard work built on it, at a cost that grows as more is built. *[from ax:first-review, ax:unknown, d:incumbent, d:polls, p:incumbent-start, ind:reversal-grows, d:reversal, ax:rework]* {#p:early-polls}
1. **Proposition.** A rival wins a voter from an incumbent only with an advantage worth more to that voter than their share of the reversal's cost. The advantage a rival needs grows with each decision built on the incumbent, while keeping the incumbent needs none. *[from ax:prefer, ind:reversal-grows, p:incumbent-start, d:incumbent, d:reversal, d:does-better, ax:rework]* {#p:challenger-bar}
1. **Lemma.** A direction poll asks voters only to agree that work should continue, less than a forwarding poll asks, so it can pass where a forwarding poll would fail. Yet passing it in a design room makes the design the incumbent. *[from d:polls, d:incumbent, f:design-forward, ax:prefer, p:early-polls]* {#l:poll-wording}
1. **Proposition.** If a poll places a proposal among the work the committee aims to include in the next IS, the proposal joins a category SD-4 has chairs take up first. A meeting's hours are fixed, so the time it then gets comes out of the time left for papers outside those categories. *[from f:prioritization, ax:hours]* {#p:roadmap-poll}
1. **Proposition.** Close study takes longer the bigger a design is, and a bigger design has more places where a defect can go unnoticed. For a reviewer whose stake does not grow with size, close study therefore stops being worth its time sooner on a big design than on a small one. *[from ax:review-cost, ax:attention, ax:unknown, ax:prefer, d:stake, d:does-better]* {#p:review-share}
1. **Proposition.** Those who have not read a paper or followed its discussion typically abstain, so a proposal costing more to read than most stakes in it is voted on mainly by those with large stakes. Unless those of them who object cast at least a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes, it can advance with no change at all. *[from f:not-vote, p:review-share, ax:review-cost, ax:prefer, l:vote-count, p:least-cost-response]* {#p:light-review}
1. **Proposition.** A design needs the 2:1 norm to advance, and once adopted, a paper or ballot comment to remove it needs the same, switching the default to keeping it. After a successful final ballot, design changes are expected to have near-unanimous consent, ISO bars technical changes at approval, and SD-4 calls published features much harder to update incompatibly. *[from f:two-to-one, f:no-paper, f:comment-content, f:near-unanimous, f:late-changes, f:is-hard-to-change, d:reversal, d:follows-norm]* {#p:burden-flip}
1. **Proposition.** An objector who arrives after a design has been adopted faces three obstacles at once. Removal needs a paper or ballot comment meeting the 2:1 norm, reversal costs more for every decision built on the design, and the record keeps its case but unwritten objections only in memory. *[from p:burden-flip, ind:reversal-grows, p:challenger-bar, p:reasons-unrecorded, p:late-objection]* {#p:late-arrival}

## 6. Sponsors, proposal size, and parts

1. **Proposition.** Each participant a sponsor pays to attend has a vote in the room and values its interests, and no rule limits how many it pays. SC22 may cap only the experts each national body or liaison appoints, and at SC22 itself each national body counts once. *[from p:who-votes, f:no-sponsor-cap, f:one-vote, ax:who-pays, f:experts-appointed, f:registered, f:expert-cap, f:pmember-consensus, ax:prefer, d:rules]* {#p:sponsor-votes}
1. **Proposition.** Changing the features a sponsor's interests depend on costs that sponsor rework of what it built on them, lost fit with its requirements, and more review, all still to come. The gain of a better design is spread across all the feature's users, and the same 2:1 norm decides every proposal, however many users will rely on it. *[from ax:prefer, ax:rework, ax:review-cost, ax:users-affected, f:two-to-one, d:sponsor]* {#p:sponsor-features}
1. **Induction.** Each part adopted adds to what a rival to the whole design must replace: the part itself and the work built on it, later parts that depend on it included. *[from m:moves-author, ind:reversal-grows, d:reversal, ax:rework]* {#ind:adopted-parts}
1. **Proposition.** Presenting a design in parts lets the first parts be adopted before the whole is reviewed, and each adopted part adds to what a rival to the whole must replace. Its costs include a paper, a presentation, and a set of polls for each part. *[from m:moves-author, f:design-forward, f:wording-forward, f:plenary-owns, ind:adopted-parts, p:challenger-bar, ax:review-cost, f:present-required]* {#p:parts}
1. **Proposition.** A first part that fixes a design's structure can be adopted before reviewers can study in detail what later parts need of it, and then every rival to that structure must replace it. SD-4 asks no paper to state those needs, so a design-bound author's best first paper is the smallest part that fixes the structure and still meets the 2:1 norm against opponents who foresee the rest. *[from ax:first-review, ax:attention, ax:prefer, d:stake, f:no-parts-step, p:parts, ind:adopted-parts, p:challenger-bar, l:vote-count, d:design-bound]* {#p:first-part}
1. **Proposition.** A large proposal has more sponsored authors in the room than a small one, and is expected to face more meetings where a poll can make it the incumbent or end its incumbency. *[from d:large, d:small, d:incumbent, p:sponsor-votes]* {#p:large-position}
1. **Proposition.** A small proposal's author bears every meeting's cost alone, and outside SD-4's priority categories the proposal gets agenda time only after the priority work. Where a study group incubates proposals on its topic, a small proposal can start there. *[from d:small, ax:cost, f:prioritization, f:agenda-papers, f:sg-initial-proposal, d:rooms]* {#p:small-position}
1. **Proposition.** Each result in this chapter about large or small proposals rests on an attribute their definitions name: who pays the authors' costs, the number of authors, review's expected span, or the paper's length. So it holds for any proposal with that attribute, whatever its size. *[from d:small, d:large, p:large-position, p:small-position]* {#p:size-attributes}

## 7. Study groups and deadlocks

1. **Proposition.** On a topic with a study group, rival designs can be polled there first, among those who chose that room over the rooms meeting with it. The design it sends on is where the design room starts, and the design room, which owns selecting designs, can still poll a rival that has a paper. *[proved by l:sg-purpose, l:sg-attendance, l:sg-start]* *[from f:vehicle, f:multiple-polls]* {#p:sg-decides}
   1. **Lemma.** SD-4 has the convenor form a study group on a design room chair's recommendation when a topic has broad interest, no unified initial proposal, and a strong chair candidate. It gives the study group the role of producing an initial proposal that can then enter the design room. *[from f:sg-formation, f:sg-initial-proposal]* {#l:sg-purpose}
   1. **Lemma.** A participant can attend only one of the rooms meeting at the same time, and votes and speaks only there. So a study group's voters are those for whom its topic outweighs the rooms meeting with it. *[from ax:parallel-rooms, ax:prefer, d:stake, f:one-vote]* {#l:sg-attendance}
   1. **Lemma.** The design room starts from the study group's initial proposal, which a rival without a paper does not block while it is an active proposal making progress. *[from l:sg-purpose, l:sg-attendance, f:no-wait-alternatives, f:sg-initial-proposal]* {#l:sg-start}
1. **Proposition.** Paying participants to attend a study group puts a sponsor's voters where the design the design room starts from is chosen. Where the other voters cast fewer Favor-plus-Against votes, fewer paid participants make up a third of the total, enough to block, or over two thirds, enough to meet the norm. *[from p:sg-decides, p:sponsor-votes, l:vote-count, d:follows-norm]* {#p:sg-sponsor}
1. **Proposition.** When each of two groups casts at least a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes against the other's design, neither design meets the 2:1 norm. The deadlock lasts until voters change position, a design changes, the voters present change, a chair calls consensus below the norm, or the design room sends both to competing TSs. *[from l:vote-count, f:two-to-one, f:design-forward, f:vehicle, f:competing-ts]* {#p:deadlock}
1. **Proposition.** In a long deadlock, a group whose sponsors keep paying its costs bears less per meeting than a group paying its own way. In the case the table below describes, the first does better holding and the second yielding, whichever design is better. *[proved by l:hold-yield-holder-gains, l:hold-yield-both-hold, l:hold-yield-whole-over-half, l:hold-yield-half-over-deadlock, l:hold-yield-half-over-none, l:hold-yield-cheap-holds, l:hold-yield-costly-yields]* *[from ax:who-pays, ax:cost, p:deadlock]* {#p:attrition}
   1. **Lemma.** When one deadlocked group yields, the other holds, and no one else votes Against the holder's design, the deadlock ends with that design advancing. The holder gains its whole stake, the yielder none of its own, and neither bears the cost of holding through a deadlock. *[from p:deadlock, l:vote-count, d:stake]* {#l:hold-yield-holder-gains}
   1. **Lemma.** When both groups hold and the deadlock ends with either design equally likely, each can expect half its stake on average, less its cost of holding through it. A group's participants whose costs sponsors pay bear less of that cost per meeting. *[from p:deadlock, ax:cost, ax:who-pays, d:stake]* {#l:hold-yield-both-hold}
   1. **Lemma.** Each group does better holding while the other yields, gaining its whole stake at no cost of holding, than when both yield to a compromise worth half its stake. *[from l:hold-yield-holder-gains, ax:prefer, d:does-better]* {#l:hold-yield-whole-over-half}
   1. **Lemma.** Each group does better when both yield to a compromise worth half its stake than when both hold. That holds because a deadlock both hold brings half its stake on average, less a cost of holding. *[from l:hold-yield-both-hold, ax:cost, ax:prefer, d:does-better]* {#l:hold-yield-half-over-deadlock}
   1. **Lemma.** Each group does better when both yield to a compromise worth half its stake than when it yields while the other holds, which brings none of its stake. *[from l:hold-yield-holder-gains, ax:prefer, d:does-better]* {#l:hold-yield-half-over-none}
   1. **Lemma.** A group whose cost of holding is less than half its stake does better holding through a deadlock both hold than yielding while the other holds. 1. **Theorem.** Under the rules and given the axioms, these choices form a settled pattern. A design-bound author seeks direction polls as early as they can pass, proposes first the smallest part that fixes the features it needs and can pass, and chooses the IS where it can pass. It leaves out a trade-off no reviewer would raise when stating it costs more than later exposure would, and answers objections with the cheapest change that keeps the design passing. A result-seeking author states trade-offs and makes changes whenever the better design they bring outweighs the votes or work they cost. It backs a rival that serves users better by more than its share of the reversal's cost. An author who values both chooses, at each two-way choice, as the type whose value differs more. A small proposal's author keeps it presented while its chance of adoption is worth the meetings still ahead, whose cost they bear alone. A regular votes Favor on colleagues' proposals they support, and Against one they doubt only when the change that vote can make outweighs the standing and time it costs. Otherwise the regular takes whichever of Neutral, not voting, or Favor does best, and Favor risks nothing with the colleague. A sponsor pays for participants where the decisions it values are made, study groups included. Chairs follow the 2:1 norm and the convenor SD-4's plenary procedure, unless their stake outweighs what departing can cost them. A national body whose comments fare the same under any vote votes Yes with comments unless removing one feature is worth as much to it as the rest of the document. A user without a sponsor takes part only when their own stake outweighs a full price borne alone, and otherwise uses a cheaper move or none.

   In this pattern a design's content can be fixed when it is least reviewed. Its first favorable poll can make it the incumbent, and objectors short of a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes cannot force a change. Every later rival or change must then redo work built on it, at a cost that grows whether or not the design is wrong. Objections count only in fixed forms and at fixed times, and some of how the committee applies these rules is learned only by attending. In a long deadlock, the group whose sponsors keep paying its costs can hold longest, whichever design is better. A proposal that costs more to read than most participants' stakes in it can advance with no change at all. An objector who arrives after adoption needs a paper or ballot comment meeting the 2:1 norm, faces a reversal cost that has grown, and finds unwritten objections kept only in memory. Other things equal, a proposal advances more readily when its authors keep presenting it or know the procedures, or when a sponsor pays for participants who hold its position. The same holds when the chair expects fewer problems from it, a poll has placed it among the work aimed at the next IS, or it is already the incumbent. No user votes, and the needs that reach a vote are those participants and their sponsors know, which need not match those of users at large. The participants present decide content, and no rule limits how many voters one sponsor pays for. A change to a design costs the sponsors who have built on it, while its gain is spread across all users. Authors get the adoption they value at the poll, while a flaw found only in implementation and use costs users later. Technical merit enters through what participants value, including a good result for users, and through the discussion, papers, and implementation experience that inform their votes. SD-4 assigns no one to rule on it, and the consensus test never asks whether an objection is right. No SD-4 step checks whether separately adopted designs work together, review worth its cost to users together but not to any one participant goes undone, and the record need not show whether objections were answered. The rules ask no author to state their own design's drawbacks. Leaving one out costs a design-bound author no step the rules require before adoption, while stating one can cost a result-seeking author votes. A first part that fixes a structure can be adopted before reviewers can study in detail what later parts need of it, and every rival to that structure must then replace it. The rules also provide checks: a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes can block, every view must be heard, rivals with papers can be polled together, implementation experience informs votes, and national bodies ballot each draft.
