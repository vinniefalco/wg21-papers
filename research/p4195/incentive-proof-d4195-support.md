# What the WG21 incentive proof can support in D4195

This report sorts the 120 conclusions extracted from the D4195 draft into four groups: what the incentive proof already states, what it can state with added premises, what it can only predict, and what it cannot reach. It is organized around the six questions in the brief, and it tests each question rather than assuming the answer.

**Labels.** C1 to C120 are the conclusions in the conclusions file. A1 to A91 are the premises in the premises file; "r" after a premise (as in A84r) means the repaired form that file gives. The approved additions are written add-1 to add-12 in the order the brief lists them: add-1 priority category, add-2 study group sponsorship, add-3 sponsor features and costs still to come, add-4 national body risk, add-5 national body oversight, add-6 what decides content, add-7 integration, add-8 users' spread gain, add-9 disclosure, add-10 minimal first part, add-11 process fluency, add-12 two author types. Keys such as `p:early-polls` are items in the proof's foundation and spine. The conclusions file's own "premises needed" field numbers the draft's fourteen Assumptions, which is a different list from A1 to A91; this report does not use those numbers.

## Summary

| Verdict | Count |
|---|---|
| PROVED | 49 |
| PROVABLE | 35 |
| PREDICTION | 6 |
| NOT PROVABLE | 30 |

Of the 30 not provable, 14 are case histories and 14 are claims about what participants feel or think, all outside the proof by design; one is a recommendation, and one rests on a standard ("works well") the proof does not define. The 35 provable verdicts need fifteen premises from the premises file, and each folds into one of seven existing axioms, so the proof needs no new axiom item.

The most important findings:

1. **Momentum and fluency are real in the rules, but "with little review" is not a theorem.** The proof shows that the stage that settles content comes first, when review is least; that each favorable poll makes a design the one later discussion starts from; that the cost of reversal grows as more is built on a design; and, with add-11, that objections count only in the right form, channel, and time, which participants learn by attending. It cannot show that a determined author's design passes with little review. The rules allow light review, and reviewers whose stakes do not grow with a paper give it less study, but any group casting a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes can stop a design at each of several stages. How much review a given design got can only be predicted.

2. **Users are left without a vote, not without a voice, and the rewards are not "users against the room."** No user votes, and because a better design's gain is spread across all users while participating costs the one who participates, few users do better taking part. But users' interests reach decisions through every participant's own valuation of a good result for users, through TS feedback, through papers a participant presents, and through national bodies, which ISO asks to take account of all interests concerned. What the rules do is pay an author at adoption while a flaw's cost arrives later and falls on users. No one's reward under the rules depends on how a feature works after adoption.

3. **Who decides is settled, and nobody is assigned to check quality or fit.** Content is decided by the participants present, under the 2:1 norm and chairs' calls. Wording rooms check only that wording expresses the design, national bodies can accept or reject only the whole document, no one is assigned to judge design quality, and no step checks that separately adopted designs work together.

4. **Disclosure and the minimal first part hold only for some authors and some conditions.** An author whose gain depends on keeping particular features fixed does better leaving a trade-off undisclosed only when voters would not otherwise learn of it. A written answer to an objection can cut either way, because SD-4 sorts a repeated objection by whether it brings new information, which is easier to check when the earlier answer is written. A minimal first part that fixes a design's structure is the best first move only when the part is worth something alone and opponents who foresee the later parts cannot cast a third of the votes against it.

5. **The draft misstates some rules and contradicts some results.** It cites ISO clause 2.5.6 for the register of opposition; in the extract that text is in 2.5.7, it is an approach ISO "recommends" to "the leadership," and it registers the opposition rather than its arguments. The draft also cites a 2024 edition that the extract's edition replaces, and two of its quotations differ from the extract. Three conclusions contradict proof results: the record "cannot" show whether an objection was answered (the proof shows it need not), disputes end "not when one side is persuaded" (a deadlock can end when voters change position), and forwarding shifts the burden to the objector (before the final ballot, a removal advances under the same 2:1 norm as adoption; what changes is the default and each voter's cost of reversal).

## 1. Procedural momentum and process fluency

**The question tested.** Do momentum and fluency let a determined author's design pass the stages that settle its content with little review? Partly. Content is settled in the design rooms and in the study groups whose initial proposals they take up (`p:design-in-rooms`), and the first poll there comes when the design has had the least review (`ax:first-review`). An author does better with early favorable polls (`p:early-polls`), a design that wins one becomes the incumbent that a rival without a paper cannot block (`p:incumbent-start`), and the cost of reversal does not fall as the design advances (`ind:reversal-grows`), and grows after each favorable poll once A29 is added. With add-11 and the fluency premises, the proof can show that knowing the procedures raises a participant's odds other things equal. What it cannot show is "with little review" as a general result: the 2:1 norm lets any group casting a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes stop a design (`l:one-third-against`) at the study group, the design room, the wording room's referral back, and plenary, and the rules contain further checks (`p:checks`). The strongest version: the stage that fixes the starting design comes when the least is known, nothing in the rules scales review to the stakes, and reviewers whose stakes do not grow with a paper give it less study. The determined author is add-12's design-bound author; the rules give it the same moves as a result-seeking author. Additions that apply here: add-1, add-2, add-3, add-11, add-12.

**Already shown (12).**

- **C6** (disputes end when the minority stops bearing the cost, not by persuasion). PROVED in corrected form; the draft's wording contradicts the spine in part. Keys `p:advance-unanswered`, `p:deadlock`, `p:attrition`, `p:stake-threshold`. Strongest version: a proposal can advance without persuading objectors who cast less than a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes, so their willingness to keep opposing does not decide the outcome; where each side can stop the other, the contest lasts until voters change position (which includes being persuaded), a design changes, the voters present change, a chair calls consensus below the norm, or the designs go to competing TSs, and a side whose costs a sponsor pays bears less per meeting.
- **C25** (a rival is treated as an amendment and must beat the cost of reversal; merit still decides). PROVED for the cost bar. Keys `p:challenger-bar`, `p:incumbent-start`, `ind:reversal-grows`. Overstated: the bar is per voter and covers only the part of the cost of reversal that falls on what that voter values. The rules let a chair poll a rival with a paper side by side (`f:multiple-polls`), so whether it is put as an amendment is a choice of poll, which C91's prediction covers.
- **C31** (an early mistake gets harder to correct, not less wrong). PROVED. Keys `ind:reversal-grows`, `p:challenger-bar`, `p:burden-flip`. The cost of reversal does not fall and does not depend on whether the design is wrong, and the threshold rises at two points: near-unanimous consent after a successful final ballot, and, once published, what SD-4 calls "much more difficult" (`f:is-hard-to-change`).
- **C37** (publish, attend, persist, compromise selectively, build standing, revise until no organized opposition remains). PROVED in corrected form. Keys `l:settled-large-author`, `l:settled-small-author`, `p:least-cost-response`, `thm:settled`. Overstated: the norm needs more than twice as many Favor as Against votes, not the absence of opposition, so the least costly change keeps the norm met and opposition under a third can remain.
- **C41** (preferring another design versus being willing to stop one). PROVED. Keys `p:prefer-vs-stop`, `p:against-costs-more`, `p:stake-threshold`, `t:vote-choice`. A participant can state a preference and vote Neutral, which leaves the count unchanged and draws no questions, while an Against vote costs more. "Much more" is a size the proof does not give, and what participants "learn" is outside it.
- **C43** (a strong position without a champion is weak). PROVED. Keys `f:present-required`, `f:no-wait-alternatives`, `p:late-objection`, `p:who-votes`, `f:no-merit-ruling`. A proposal without a presenter is not actionable, an alternative without a paper "does not exist," an objection counts only through votes present or escalation in time, and no step tests quality, so a weaker design with present supporters can meet the norm.
- **C50** (a late objection from someone new asks the room to give up prior work and time). PROVED in rule form. Keys `p:challenger-bar`, `ind:reversal-grows`, `p:late-objection`, `p:nb-route`, `l:nb-comment-limits`. A late objection prevails only if each voter values it above that voter's share of the cost of reversal, and SD-4 gives no plenary weight to one not escalated by the deadline. The "trust" part concerns how participants weigh views and is outside the proof by design.
- **C57** (SD-4 creates the pressure through accumulating polls, its consensus definition, and its timeliness norm). PROVED. Keys `p:incumbent-start`, `ind:reversal-grows`, `l:consensus-test`, `p:stake-threshold`, `p:late-objection`, `p:one-meeting-cycle`, `f:ts-default`.
- **C59** (repeated Strongly Against votes are personally costly). PROVED in incentive form. Keys `p:against-costs-more`, `p:stake-threshold`, `p:doubt-outside-count`, `t:vote-choice`. Each Against vote costs a regular more than a Neutral one, so repeated Against votes come only from those for whom each is worth its cost. "Psychologically expensive" is outside the proof by design.
- **C64** (after forwarding, the objector must show the proposal should be withdrawn). PROVED in corrected form; the draft's framing conflicts with the spine. Keys `p:burden-flip`, `p:incumbent-start`, `p:challenger-bar`. Before the final ballot, a paper or national body comment proposing removal normally advances under the same 2:1 norm as adoption. What changes is the default (the design stays unless removal meets the norm) and each voter's weighing of the cost of reversal; near-unanimous consent is expected only after a successful final ballot.
- **C84** (an author does best getting favorable polls as early as possible). PROVED. Keys `p:early-polls`, `p:incumbent-start`, `ind:reversal-grows`, `l:settled-large-author`. The spine states it with its condition: unless the changes later review would bring cost the author more than early incumbency gains. "Far more than the question asked" is a size the proof does not give.
- **C92** (persistence raises a design's chance apart from its quality). PROVED. Keys `p:attrition`, `p:deadlock`, `t:hold-or-yield`, `c:lever-deadlock-rule`, `f:present-required`. In a contest where each side can stop the other, the side that can bear attending longer has an advantage unrelated to quality. Outside such contests, persistence matters because an unpresented proposal is not actionable, but the proposal still needs the 2:1 norm.

**Can be shown with added premises (22).**

- **C3** (fluency, the cost of reversal, and the record reinforce one another). PROVABLE with A13, A20, A58, A59, A62, A67 and add-11. (1) Proposals and objections have weight only in the right form, channel, and time, which participants learn partly, and in part only, by attending (add-11, `ax:learning` with A13, A58, A59, A62). (2) Each decision built on a design adds to the cost of reversal, which a rival must exceed voter by voter (`ind:reversal-grows`, `p:challenger-bar`). (3) An objection's argument stays in the public record only as its holder's paper, while the author's paper stays for decades and later participants learn only from the record (`p:dissent-needs-paper`, `p:reasons-unrecorded`, A20, A67). (4) A participant who arrives late meets all three at once. Overstated: the proof shows the three effects point the same way for a late arrival, not that they multiply.
- **C19** (procedural knowledge and expertise go together because doing the work builds knowledge). PROVABLE with A13, A19. (1) Procedural knowledge comes partly from attending (`ax:learning`), and doing the work means attending or finding a presenter (`f:present-required`). (2) Skill at design is a different ability (A19). (3) So a participant who has done much work knows more procedure, whatever their design skill. Overstated: that the two abilities correlate among participants is empirical, and that the process "appears" to select for quality is about what observers think.
- **C20** (a capable newcomer and a less capable regular face very different odds, and SD-4's steps cannot tell them apart). PROVABLE with A9, A13, A19, A58 and add-6, add-11. (1) A newcomer has none of the learning earlier meetings give (`p:newcomer`), and, other things equal, knowing the procedures raises the chance of adoption and lowers the effort of trying (A13, A58), separately from design skill (A19). (2) SD-4's steps test timing, presenter, votes, and the chair's call, and no one is assigned to judge design quality (`f:late-paper`, `f:present-required`, `l:chair-role`, add-6, add-11). (3) Voters can judge design skill only as far as quality can be judged at the poll (A9). Overstated: "radically different" is a size the proof cannot give, and "cannot tell apart" becomes "no step is assigned to tell them apart; voters can, to the extent quality is visible at the poll."
- **C26** (authors of large, sponsor-funded proposals have the most reason to raise the cost of reversal early and resist late changes). PROVABLE with A29 and add-3, add-12. (1) A design-bound author's gain depends on keeping particular features fixed, and a sponsor's interests can depend on those features (add-12, add-3). (2) After each favorable poll more is built on the design, so the cost of reversal grows, counted only as costs still to come (A29, `ind:reversal-grows`, add-3). (3) Such an author does better with early favorable polls, with parts, and with the least costly change (`p:early-polls`, `p:parts`, `p:least-cost-response`). (4) The more users and later features rely on a design, the more work a reversal must redo, users' code included (`ax:rework`, `ax:users-affected`). Overstated: the draft states this by proposal size and counts years already spent (A23 fails tests 2 and 5, A24 fails tests 1 and 5); the proof states it by attribute, for any proposal that has them (`p:size-attributes`).
- **C29** (the proposals that most need scrutiny get the least). PROVABLE with A27 (A26 is covered by `ax:review-cost`). (1) A longer or more complex paper takes more time to review (`ax:review-cost`). (2) A reviewer whose stake does not grow with the paper gives it less close study (`p:review-share`). (3) A design with more parts and interactions has more places where a defect can go unnoticed (A27). (4) So the designs with the most hiding places get the least study per part from those reviewers. Overstated: it holds for reviewers whose stake does not grow with the paper; `c:pred-abstention-length` tests it.
- **C30** (the decisions hardest to undo are made when the least is known). PROVABLE with A9, A29. (1) At first presentation a design has had the least review and cannot be fully judged (`ax:first-review`, A9). (2) An author who values adoption does better with early favorable direction polls (`p:early-polls`). (3) After each such poll more is built on the design, so the cost of reversal grows (A29, `ind:reversal-grows`). Overstated in timing: the cost of reversal is lowest at the first poll and grows after it; what happens when the least is known is the choice of the starting design, not the greatest cost.
- **C36** (the process favors persistence, paid costs, procedural knowledge, support, the chair's confidence, momentum, fit with active groups, and content that is hard to review). PROVABLE with A13, A61 (A33 covered) and add-2, add-11. Other things equal, a proposal advances more readily when its authors keep presenting it (`f:present-required`), when a sponsor pays for participants who hold its position (`p:sponsor-votes`, add-2), when its authors know the procedures (A13, add-11), when the chair expects fewer problems from it (A61), when it already holds incumbent status (`p:incumbent-start`), and when it serves what the participants present value (`p:who-votes`, `ax:prefer`). Overstated: "in practice" is a prediction, and hard-to-review content is not shown to help, since a hard paper loses informed votes on both sides (`p:review-share`, `f:not-vote`); whether it loses more doubters than supporters depends on who has stakes in it, which `c:pred-abstention-length` can test.
- **C75** (a newcomer's good work can stall for reasons of form and timing). PROVABLE with A13, A58. (1) A newcomer lacks the learning earlier meetings give (`p:newcomer`). (2) A paper not on time is not actionable, a topic without an on-time paper is off the agenda, and work outside SD-4's priorities gets time only after priority work (`f:late-paper`, `f:agenda-notice`, `p:small-position`). (3) A participant who knows the procedures spends less effort getting a paper considered (A58). Overstated: "wrong group" and "lacked motivation" are not gates in the quoted rules; timing and presentation are.
- **C76** (equal formal rights, unequal knowledge of the moves). PROVABLE with A59. (1) Every participant has the same moves (`m:moves`, `p:who-votes`). (2) A participant can use only the moves they know (A59). (3) Knowledge of the moves comes partly from attending (`ax:learning`, `p:newcomer`). SD-4 expects every participant to know its contents (`f:familiar`), so part of this knowledge is written down.
- **C77** (knowing the procedures lets a participant shape which question a poll settles). PROVABLE with A59 and add-11. (1) A chair may take any poll and an author can ask for one (`f:chair-polls`, `m:moves-author`). (2) A direction poll asks voters to accept less than a forwarding poll, so a voter can favor one and not the other (`l:poll-wording`, `ax:prefer`). (3) A participant who knows which poll to ask for, and when, can affect which question is put (A59, add-11). Overstated: the chair, not the participant, decides the poll; what a result is later taken to mean is outside the proof by design.
- **C78** (chairs reasonably prefer fluent authors because their papers pose less risk). PROVABLE with A61. (1) Advancing a proposal over Against votes risks later national body opposition, and declining delays work SD-4 says polls should favor (`p:chair-costs`). (2) A chair expects fewer problems from a paper whose author has a record of following the procedures and answering review (A61). (3) So between papers otherwise equal, the chair does better taking up first the one expected to make progress (`ax:prefer`, `f:prioritization`). Restated by attribute: the preference attaches to the record, not the person.
- **C79** (repeated attendance teaches which objections count, which decisions can be reopened, and how much revision is enough). PROVABLE with A13, A62 and add-11. (1) Some knowledge of how the committee works is unwritten and gained only by taking part (A62). (2) Whether an objection is timely, new, or serious decides its weight (add-11, `f:timely`, `p:repeat-objection`, `p:late-objection`). (3) Other things equal, that knowledge improves a participant's odds (A13). SD-4 writes part of it down: new information, timeliness, and rarity.
- **C80** (fluency, chair confidence, agenda time, and success feed one another). PROVABLE in weaker form with A13, A61 and add-1, add-11. (1) Knowing the procedures raises the chance of adoption (A13). (2) A record of following them lowers the chair's expected cost of advancing a paper (A61, `p:chair-costs`). (3) Work that can make plenary progress, and work placed among what the committee aims to include in the next IS, gets agenda time first (`f:prioritization`, `l:plenary-priority`, add-1). (4) Attending adds learning (`ax:learning`). Overstated: each link pushes the same way, so the advantage does not shrink with meetings attended, but that it compounds is not shown.
- **C82** (the key difference is knowing when a decision is made and what must already have happened, not the right to vote). PROVABLE with A13, A59 and add-11. (1) Every participant present has one vote (`f:one-vote`). (2) Objections need escalation by the deadline, rivals need on-time papers, and decided objections need new information (add-11, `p:late-objection`, `f:no-wait-alternatives`, `p:repeat-objection`). (3) Content is settled in design rooms and study groups, which a participant must attend at the time (`p:design-in-rooms`, `p:concern-presence`). (4) Knowing this comes partly from attending (A59, `ax:learning`). Overstated: "the key difference" is a ranking the proof cannot make; it shows a difference that does not depend on voting rights.
- **C83** (a poll works less as information than as a change of position). PROVABLE with A29. (1) A design that passes a direction poll becomes the incumbent, which a rival without a paper does not block (`d:incumbent`, `p:incumbent-start`). (2) More is then built on it (A29), so the cost of reversal grows (`ind:reversal-grows`). (3) A rival must exceed each voter's share of that cost (`p:challenger-bar`). Overstated: "less as information than" is a comparison the proof does not make; every favorable poll does both.
- **C85** (a poll that only encourages work still builds momentum). PROVABLE with A29. (1) A direction poll asks voters to accept less than a forwarding poll (`l:poll-wording`), so it passes more easily. (2) Passing it makes the design the incumbent (`d:incumbent`, `p:incumbent-start`). (3) More is then built on it and the cost of reversal grows (A29, `ind:reversal-grows`). So an encouraging poll has effects beyond its question.
- **C93** (an experienced author revises or waits rather than force an immediate vote between designs). PROVABLE with no new premise (A73 is covered by `ax:prefer`). (1) A failed direction poll ends a design's standing as incumbent (`d:incumbent`). (2) An author who values adoption and expects to lose does better revising and waiting, since each later meeting is another chance (`l:settled-large-author`, `f:agenda-papers`). (3) The chair decides which polls to take (`f:chair-polls`). "Usually" is a frequency and only predictable.
- **C97** (when serious review is cheap, authors revise, reviewers scrutinize, and the process works). PROVABLE with no new premise (A77 is covered). (1) A reviewer whose stake exceeds the cost of study studies the paper (`ax:prefer`, `p:review-share`). (2) Informed Against votes that threaten the 2:1 norm make a change worth more to the author than its cost (`p:least-cost-response`, `p:design-changes`). Overstated: "works as intended" has no meaning in the proof, and the draft's matrix payoffs are its own.
- **C98** (when serious review is costly, authors push unchanged and reviewers abstain). PROVABLE with no new premise (A77 is covered). (1) A reviewer whose stake is below the cost of study does not study and typically does not vote (`p:review-share`, `f:not-vote`). (2) With too few informed Against votes to stop the norm, the author's least costly response is no change or a confined one (`p:least-cost-response`). (3) So a proposal whose review costs more than reviewers' stakes advances with little change. Overstated: "without adequate review" needs a standard the proof lacks, the claim about intermediate costs uses mixed strategies outside the proof's model, and SD-4 says a Neutral majority can be concerning (`l:neutral-outside`).
- **C101** (the chair and the room face a coordination problem that early signals settle). PROVABLE with no new premise (A79 is covered). (1) Below a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes, the author's least costly answer to objectors is no change or a confined one (`p:least-cost-response`). (2) At a third or more, the norm fails until a design changes enough for Against voters to accept it (`l:one-third-against`, `p:design-changes`). (3) So each objector's Against vote is worth more when enough others also vote Against, and patterns in which the objectors all oppose and in which none do can both be settled. Overstated: the proof does not show which pattern arises or that early signals select it, and why SD-4 asks for early objections is a claim about the rules' purpose, outside the proof.
- **C102** (at plenary the room has settled on advancing, and a late opponent must move all of it). PROVABLE with A29. (1) Plenary does not redesign and gives no weight to objections not escalated by the deadline (`p:plenary-ratifies`, `p:late-objection`). (2) Each decision built on the design raises the cost of reversal (A29, `ind:reversal-grows`). (3) A late opponent must win each voter by more than that voter's share of the cost (`p:challenger-bar`). "Usually settled" would need one more fact from the extract, SD-4's statement of plenary that "Most polls pass by unanimous consent."
- **C108** (an experienced author of a large proposal seeks scheduling, the chair's confidence, early direction polls, and a study group's time before investing). PROVABLE with A61 (A83r is covered) and add-1, add-2. (1) An author and sponsor prefer to learn whether the committee supports the direction before most of the cost is spent (A83r, covered by `ax:prefer`, `ax:cost`, `ax:unknown`). (2) Early favorable direction polls make the design the incumbent (`p:early-polls`). (3) A poll placing the work among what the committee aims to include in the next IS moves it up the agenda (add-1), and the chair's expectation of fewer problems helps (A61). (4) A sponsor does better paying for participation in a study group whose output it values (add-2). Overstated: the draft's verb for this fails tests 1 and 4; the proof states it as seeking, through moves the rules give every author.

**Only predictable (2).**

- **C91** (the language moves from two competing designs to a status quo and a request to reopen, even when nothing technical changed). PREDICTION. Evidence: poll question texts and paper titles over the life of a contest. Innocent explanation: the words track a real change of procedural status, which the proof shows happens with each poll whatever the technical content (`d:incumbent`, `p:burden-flip`). Data that would tell the two apart: whether chairs polled a rival that had a paper side by side, as SD-4 encourages (`f:multiple-polls`), or only as a change to the incumbent, and whether rivals drew fewer Favor votes in the second form on comparable topics.
- **C94** (a losing design is rarely rejected outright; it gets less time, no forwarding poll, more revision requests, and then its advocate stops). PREDICTION, the spine's `c:pred-small-fade`. Evidence: mailings and poll results, counting designs whose last paper drew no negative poll. Innocent explanation: authors drop ideas on reflection, or after a better design appears. Data that would tell the two apart: such stops compared between authors who pay their own costs and sponsored authors with papers of similar length, and between topics with and without an incumbent. The mechanism itself is proved: an author stops when pursuing is no longer worth the cost, and work outside the priorities gets time last (`l:settled-small-author`, `p:small-position`).

**Cannot be shown (5).** All five are case histories, outside the proof by design.

- **C13** (the contracts case followed the ideal-author playbook). The general form is already a spine prediction: proposals whose authors' costs are paid stop less often without a negative poll (`c:pred-small-fade`).
- **C16** (in the networking case a direction poll set the framework months before a concrete proposal). A general form could become a prediction: direction polls often come before the first concrete paper for the approach they favor. Evidence: poll dates and mailing dates. Innocent explanation: a direction poll is how authors learn whether to invest (A83r). Telling data: whether a concrete rival paper existed at the time, and how often such early direction polls were later reversed.
- **C66** (in the contracts case the main objection papers arrived after forwarding). A general form could become a prediction: objection papers filed after a forwarding poll succeed less often than those filed before it. Evidence: mailing dates and poll results. Innocent explanation: later papers more often repeat concerns already weighed. Telling data: whether the late papers bring new information, such as new implementation experience.
- **C67** (a study group chair's later account of the pace). Also a claim about what participants felt. A general form about pace (meetings between first presentation and forwarding, set against Against counts) could become a prediction, but it would not reach the feelings.
- **C95** (in the contracts case a competing design was votable only as an amendment). The general form joins C91's prediction.

## 2. How C++ users are left out of the decisions

**The question tested.** True as to votes, overstated as to influence. No user votes in any poll or ballot (`p:who-votes`, `m:moves-user`). A user does better participating only when their stakes in the decisions they could join exceed the cost (`l:settled-user`), and add-8 with A8 shows why that rarely holds: a better design's gain is spread across all users, the cost falls on the one who participates, and one user's effort seldom changes an outcome. But "left out" is too strong. Users' interests enter every vote through each participant's own valuation of a good result for users (`ax:prefer`), and users have cheaper moves: TS or white paper feedback, a paper a participant presents, a request to a national body, which ISO asks to take account of all interests concerned at the national level (`f:iso-early`), or becoming a participant. The weak point is the national body channel, since a national body states a position only where forming one is worth its members' time (C107, `c:pred-nb-positions`). What the proof cannot yet say is how much weight these indirect channels have; that would need a new prediction, and the spine has none on it. Additions that apply: add-5, add-6, add-8.

**Already shown (2).**

- **C39** (the committee hears from those who can afford to take part and delivers to users with no structural means of influence). PROVED in corrected form. ## 3. What the incentives reward, and who decides what goes into the standard

**The question tested.** "Who decides" has a direct answer, which add-6 states: content is decided by the participants present, under the 2:1 norm and chairs' calls; wording rooms check only that wording expresses the design; national bodies can accept or reject only the whole document (`p:path`, `p:chair-decides`, `p:bundling`, `l:chair-role`). On rewards, the brief sets users, the language, and quality against the room, its sponsors, and its authors. The proof does not support that split. It shows that the rules pay an author at adoption while a flaw's cost arrives later and can fall on any user (`p:reward-timing`), that a sponsor adds votes by paying for participants (`p:sponsor-votes`, `l:settled-sponsor`), and that no rule attaches a reward to how a feature works after adoption. But every participant values a good result for users along with adoption, standing, a sponsor's interests, and their own time (`ax:prefer`), and that value enters every vote. The strongest version: quality is rewarded only through what participants value and what they can see at the poll, and nothing in the rules ties anyone's gain to the feature's later performance. Additions that apply: add-3, add-4, add-6, add-8, add-12.

## 4. Quality control and integration

**The question tested.** True in the form the approved additions state, and the proof already shows its core. No one is assigned to judge design quality (`f:no-merit-ruling`), the consensus test does not ask whether an objection is correct (`l:consensus-test`), wording rooms check only that wording expresses the design (`f:wording-forward`, add-6), and the record need not show whether objections were answered (`p:record-indistinct`). On integration, add-7 stands, but the extract holds three passages its absence fact should name and set aside: SD-4 asks that a good paper "Show how the proposed solution fits with the rest of the language's principles and design philosophy"; it says an early TS merge permits better "integration testing" in the full context of the IS; and it has all new wording written against the adopted working drafts. ## 5. Whether authors do better leaving trade-offs undisclosed

**The question tested.** Not shown as a general claim; shown in conditional form. The rules require no paper to state its design's costs. SD-4 says a good paper "should" show the design alternatives considered and why they were not pursued (`f:show-alternatives`), and an author who declines a directed change should defend that choice with new information (`f:direction-implement`), but neither is a condition for a paper to be considered (`f:late-paper`, `f:present-required`), and no record must show how objections were answered (`p:record-indistinct`). With add-9's premise that what a paper discloses can change votes, and add-12's two author types, the proof can show three things. A design-bound author does better leaving a trade-off undisclosed when disclosure can only cost Favor votes and voters would not otherwise learn of it. A trade-off that reviewers or a rival will raise anyway gives no author anything to gain from silence, since under add-9's premise votes move only with what voters learn. A result-seeking author can do better disclosing, since a better-informed vote can yield a design that serves users better. The draft also misses a second side: SD-4 sorts a repeated objection by whether it brings new information (`f:ballot-new-info`), and only a written record lets anyone check what is new, so a written answer can make a decision harder to reopen, not easier. The draft says little about trade-offs directly; the conclusions placed here concern written answers and framing. Additions that apply: add-9, add-12.

## 6. Whether the best strategy is a minimal first part that fixes a design's structure

**The question tested.** True only under conditions, which add-10 names. The proof shows that presenting a large design in parts lets the first parts be adopted before the whole is reviewed, and that every rival to the whole must then replace each adopted part (`p:parts`, `ind:adopted-parts`). With A84r and A29, it can show that a first part that fixes the structure draws study in proportion to its apparent effect, and becomes costlier to replace as more is built on it. It cannot show that this is the best strategy in general. It is the best first move when the part is worth something alone, and when opponents who foresee the later parts cannot cast a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes against it (`l:one-third-against`, add-10). SD-4's request that a good paper state its design principles and alternatives (`f:show-alternatives`) can also expose the structure early. The draft's claim that the first part "forecloses competing designs" states an aim, which fails test 4 (A84); the proof states an effect on a rival's costs. The draft's term for the strategy, "minimum viable lock-in," should be read as a first part whose adoption raises the cost of reversal for every rival. Additions that apply: add-3, add-10, add-12.

**Already shown (2).**

- **C112** (once adopted, the first part makes a rival compete against it, its support, and its planned extensions). PROVED. Keys `ind:adopted-parts`, `p:challenger-bar`, `p:parts`. Overstated: planned extensions not yet adopted are not part of the cost of reversal; they weigh only with voters who value them.
- **C113** (the authors then point to prior consensus and oppose reopening). PROVED in rule form. Keys `p:repeat-objection`, `p:burden-flip`, `f:pre-merge`, `f:ballot-decided`. The rules let authors oppose reopening that brings no new information. "Investment" should read as costs still to come (add-3).

**Can be shown with added premises (3).**

- **C109** (the author's best strategy is the smallest adoptable part whose adoption makes every rival bear the cost of reversal). PROVABLE in conditional form with A29, A84r (A85 covered) and add-3, add-10. (1) A poll on a whole design draws Against votes from every voter who opposes any part strongly enough, while a first part can be adopted before the whole is reviewed (`p:stake-threshold`, `p:parts`). (2) Reviewers of an early part cannot see what later parts will need of it, and they study a proposal in proportion to its apparent effect (A84r, A85). (3) Once adopted, the part is something every rival must replace, and more is built on it (`ind:adopted-parts`, A29). (4) So a first part that fixes the structure is the author's best first move unless it is worth little alone, or opponents who foresee the later parts can cast a third of the votes against it (add-10). Overstated: "best strategy" in general, and "forecloses" as an aim.
- **C111** (the first part passes beneath the review threshold). PROVABLE with A84r (A85 covered). (1) Reviewers study a proposal in proportion to the stake they can see (A85, `p:review-share`, `d:stake`). (2) Reviewers of an early part cannot see what later parts will require of it (A84r). (3) So the part's apparent stake is below its effect on later designs, and it draws less study than that effect warrants. Overstated: it does not hold for opponents who foresee the later parts (add-10), or for participants whose stake is in the topic itself (`l:sg-attendance`).
- **C115** (competing designs can be ruled out before the room has evaluated the full architecture). PROVABLE with A9, A84r and add-10. (1) A first part can be adopted before the whole is reviewed (`p:parts`). (2) Its reviewers cannot see the later parts and cannot fully judge the design (A84r, A9). (3) Once it is adopted, every rival must replace it (`ind:adopted-parts`). So rivals bear the cost of reversal before the full design is evaluated, unless opponents foresee it (add-10).

**Cannot be shown (1).**

- **C114** (authors come to treat the committee as an obstacle course rather than a partner). Outside the proof by design, as a claim about how authors regard the committee. A piece of it could become a proved absence with a new searched fact: no step in SD-4 asks the room what a part rules out for later designs; SD-4 asks only for alternatives considered.

## Conflicts

Places where the draft cites a rule differently from the extract, or where a draft conclusion contradicts a proof result.

1. **Clause number.** The draft (Key Judgment 3, section 1, and its reference list) cites ISO/IEC Directives clause 2.5.6 for sustained opposition, the register, and good-faith resolution. In the extract, 2.5.6 says only "Consideration of successive drafts shall continue until a consensus of the P-members as judged by the Chair has been obtained, or a decision to abandon or defer the project has been made." All of the text the draft quotes is in 2.5.7. Affects C21, C33, C49. The draft should cite 2.5.7 (high confidence: the extract places the text there).
2. **Edition and wording.** The draft's reference gives the Directives' consolidated ISO Supplement of 2024. The extract's edition "replaces the ISO/IEC Directives, Part 1:2024, including the ISO, IEC and JTC 1 Supplements." Two of the draft's quotations differ from the extract: the draft has "(i.e. in the minutes, records, etc.)" where the extract has "(i.e. in the report/minutes, records, etc.)", and the draft has "it is required to try and resolve it in good faith" where the extract has "it shall try and resolve it in good faith." The draft also cites the SD-4 revision of 2024-12-30, while the extract quotes the revision dated 2026-05-11. The draft should quote the edition the proof quotes, or say which text was in force for the period it analyzes (high confidence: the proof's facts must match the extract word for word).
3. **Recommended, given to "the leadership," and registering the opposition.** The draft says ISO's "next sentences put opposition in the minutes and require a good-faith attempt to resolve it." In the extract, "the following approach is recommended when a sustained opposition is declared." The leadership registers an opposition it finds not to be sustained, and registers it while continuing the work in the cases 2.5.7 describes; it registers "the opposition," not its arguments or an answer to it. At the committee stage the chair of SC22 judges consensus, and the text does not say whether a working group convenor applies these steps inside the group (`l:iso-register-level`). So "SD-4 does not require the register" is true, but ISO does not clearly require it of WG21's own polls either. No searched ISO clause requires a record of why consensus was judged to exist (`f:iso-no-reason-record`), so the draft's three-part "reconciliation" (objection, response, recorded finding) is the draft's own standard, not ISO's (high confidence: the extract's wording is plain).
4. **"Cannot show" against "need not show."** The draft's abstract says the required record "cannot show whether an objection was answered or outvoted," and its premise 14 says SD-4 requires no more than a tally and an outcome. SD-4 requires every meeting to be minuted, ISO requires decisions within 48 hours and minutes within four weeks, plenary may ask a subgroup chair to summarize the reason for a decision, and ISO requires a response to every enquiry ballot comment. The proof's result, `p:record-indistinct`, is that the required record need not show the answer; it may. Affects C1 (high confidence).
5. **"Important" and "sustained."** C40 reads "important" as institutional standing and "sustained" as attendance across meetings, and adds "represented." The rule text speaks of "an important part of the concerned interest," whose meaning "shall therefore be determined by the committee leadership on a case-by-case basis"; it describes interests, not a person's standing, and "represented" does not appear. "Sustained" oppositions are views "maintained by an important part of the concerned interest," and nothing in the text requires attendance across meetings. SD-4 has no step for sustained opposition at all (`f:no-sustained-procedure`), so in WG21's subgroups an objection counts through the Against votes of those present (medium confidence: "maintained" could be read to imply persistence over time, but not attendance or standing).
6. **"Veto structure."** C2 and the draft's section 2 describe a "represented veto structure." ISO says "a sustained opposition is not akin to a right to veto." Under SD-4 the only blocking position is a group casting a third of the Favor-plus-Against votes (`l:one-third-against`), and a chair may still call consensus below the norm (`p:deadlock`).
7. **Disputes end "not when one side is persuaded."** C6 contradicts `p:deadlock`, under which a contest in which each side can stop the other ends when voters change position, among other ways, and `p:advance-unanswered`, under which an objection held by less than a third does not have to be outlasted at all.
8. **The burden of proof.** C64 says that after forwarding the objector must show the proposal should be withdrawn. Under `p:burden-flip`, before the final ballot a removal normally advances under the same 2:1 norm as adoption; what shifts is the default and each voter's cost of reversal (`p:challenger-bar`), and near-unanimous consent applies only after a successful final ballot.
9. **When the cost of reversal is greatest.** C30's wording pairs the greatest cost of reversal with the least information. Under `ind:reversal-grows` the cost is lowest at the first poll and grows after it; what happens when the least is known is the choice of the starting design.
10. **"Eliminate organized opposition" and "measured agreement."** C37's strategy ends when no organized opposition remains, but the 2:1 norm lets opposition under a third remain (`p:least-cost-response`). C49 says SD-4 treats consensus as judgment "rather than measured agreement," but SD-4 does give chairs a measured norm; what it lacks is a record of reasons.
11. **No test for repeated objections.** C71 says SD-4 has no mechanism to tell a real unresolved flaw from refusal to accept the group's direction. SD-4 has a test, new information (`f:ballot-new-info`), along with timeliness and rarity (`f:escalation-allowed`, `f:timely`). What it lacks is a test of whether the objection was answered.
12. **Sunk cost and size.** C26 and Key Judgment 6 count "years of work" and state the effect by proposal size (A23 fails tests 2 and 5). The proof counts only costs still to come (add-3) and states results by attribute (`p:size-attributes`). C120's "make their own reversal impossible" conflicts with `p:burden-flip`: reversal stays open under the 2:1 norm until the final ballot, and after publication SD-4 says "much more difficult," not impossible.
13. **"Every strategy maximizes the chance of consensus."** C103 conflicts with the theorem, in which chairs follow the norm unless their stake exceeds what departing costs, reviewers save time, and national bodies vote on whole documents; only outcome-related values run through the consensus call.
14. **Users "structurally excluded."** C38's user who "optimizes for nothing" and C39's "structurally excluded" users conflict with `m:moves-user` and `l:settled-user`: users have indirect moves, and a user whose stakes exceed the cost does better participating.
